What Makes an Eruv Work

Based on Rav Hershel Schachter’s Introduction to the Modern Eruv. Tags in this section read “Intro [N]”. Turning a public area into a private one takes three things Intro [1]:

1. A border

Walls, or more practically a chain of tzurot hapetach (posts with a string on top), around the whole area. It’s the most recognizable part, and the hardest to build.

2. A rental

A deed renting the area from the local government, so non-Jewish neighbors’ rights don’t block carrying.

3. Shared food

Food the whole community owns together, usually a box of matzah in the shul. In the gemara, this food is what the word “eruv” actually means.

Why a String Isn’t Always Enough

The poskim generally assume a border of tzurot hapetach only works where the area isn’t a true, biblical reshut harabbim. The article gives two explanations.

Is the string a real wall?

The Pri Megadim suggested a tzurat hapetach is a wall only rabbinically, so it can’t convert a biblical public domain Intro [4]. The Chazon Ish disagreed: the gemara uses a tzurat hapetach to separate kil’ayim, a biblical law, so it must be a real wall Intro [5]. Defenders of the Pri Megadim answer that dividing plants only needs a divider, while a Shabbat border has to visibly enclose an area Intro [6].

A “flimsy wall”

All agree a flimsy barrier stops counting when traffic regularly crosses it. Tosafot held that where only a rabbinic wall is needed, even a flimsy one survives traffic Intro [7], and the Maharam extended this to every rabbinic case. So the consensus is that a tzurat hapetach only works where the area would otherwise be biblically a makom patur Intro [8].

      When Is a Slope a Wall?

      A natural slope counts as a halachic wall when it climbs 10 tefachim within 4 amot. Drag the slider to change the horizontal distance and watch the angle — and the verdict — change.

      This counts as a wall — it rises 10 tefachim within 4 amot.

      Why it matters

      A slope that reaches 10 tefachim of height within 4 amot of distance encloses the area above it as a reshut hayachid, same as a built wall. The top of the slope is treated as the wall — not the bottom. [28–33]

      Poskim disagree about how that 4-amot distance is measured. The majority (Rabbenu Chananel, Ritva, Mishna Brurah, Aruch Hashulchan) measure along the slope itself, giving an angle of 24.6°. A minority (Tosfot on Zevachim) measure along the flat ground below, giving a gentler 22.6°. [30]

      Make the distance more gradual than the threshold (try sliding past 4 amot) and the slope stops counting as a wall at all — per Rashi, it’s just ground people can walk over. [32]
